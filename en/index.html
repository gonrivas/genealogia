Documented genealogy · Italy and Argentina

Four surnames that happened to meet in Santa Fe

Between 1880 and 1910 a handful of families who had never heard of one another, and who had very little in common with each other, left three regions of Italy with nothing in common — Calabria, the Tuscan Garfagnana and Abruzzo. They all ended up in the same Argentine province, and their children married each other. This is an attempt to find out who they were, one document at a time, taking nothing on trust without a record to back it.

Where to start

Four branches, four corners of Europe

Each branch begins with one of Gonzalo Rivas's four grandparents and climbs backwards as far as the paper allows. They are not sealed compartments: anyone standing at a meeting point appears in several at once, which is as it should be. Choosing a branch leaves only its people, its records and its loose ends in view.

Calabria

Salerno

Rende · Marano Marchesato · Castrolibero · Cosenza (province of Cosenza)

The best-documented branch, and the one that reaches furthest back. It begins with a foundling: on 27 June 1825 a newborn was left at the foundling wheel in Cosenza and given the name Michele, which is why his paternal line stops dead there. Everything above him comes through his wife, Maria Antonia Palermo, and climbs to a Michele Volpintesta born around 1723 whose death in 1793 was copied into his granddaughter's marriage file and survives there.

90 people · 52 records Tuscany

Celeri

Trassilico · Vallico Sopra · Gallicano (Garfagnana, province of Lucca)

A mountain village in the Garfagnana, above the Serchio valley. Civil registration for Trassilico survives in two stretches — the Napoleonic one of 1808-1812 and the ducal one of 1852-1865 — with half a century of silence between them that only the parish books can cover. Desiderio Celeri emigrated from here to Argentina, and so did the paternal grandmother of the family.

63 people · 14 records Abruzzo

Faienza

Atessa · Paglieta (province of Chieti)

This branch holds the oldest person in the whole tree: Domenico Pizzi, a stonemason from Atessa, born around 1730-1740 and named only in passing in his son's death record. Four straight generations of masons. Domingo Faienza, the one who crossed the Atlantic, was born in Paglieta on 1 March 1900 — not in Atessa, as was believed here for months.

43 people · 9 records Argentina

Rivas

Santa Fe

The branch that exists in this tree entirely on family memory, without a single document behind it. It is the only one like that, and it appears here so the gap is visible. The first brick would be the birth record of Marcos César Rivas, Santa Fe, 22 March 1950.

How this was built

One rule above all: what a record says and what someone guesses are never mixed

Almost everything here comes from the Italian civil registers digitised by the Portale Antenati of the Ministry of Culture, together with parish books transcribed by local researchers and, on the Argentine side, the 1887 provincial census of Santa Fe and certificates from the civil registry.

For each record the image is kept at its original resolution — never recompressed — the Italian is transcribed exactly as written, misspellings and all, and then translated in full. After that comes a note on what it proves and what it does not, which is usually the most useful part.

Where something is a hypothesis, the text says so in those words. And when an earlier conclusion turns out to be wrong, it is not deleted: it is corrected, and the fact that it was wrong stays on the record. There is a sources file that only ever grows, with its own list of corrections, so that anyone can retrace the path and find the mistake.

What you will not find here

The two most recent generations are withheld. They are living people, and in the published version of this site their names, dates and places do not appear: they are not hidden, they are simply not in the file. They remain only as links, so the line is not broken.

You will not find noble surnames or coats of arms either. What you will find is a wide gap in fortune between one branch and another: day labourers, stonemasons, spinners and a foundling left at the wheel in Cosenza on one side, and on the other landowners, a town clerk and a lawyer from Lucca whose daughters are recorded as «possidenti». In some records people cannot sign their names; in others the godfather at the christening is the doctor from the next village.

If this is close to home

Do you think you belong to this tree?

If your surname is Salerno, Celeri, Faienza, Nelli, Rizzo, Costabile, Alasia, Palermo, Chiappetta, Volpintesta or Pizzi, or if one of your forebears left Rende, Trassilico, Atessa or Paglieta and ended up in Argentina, there is a fair chance we are on the same page of some register. Write, and we will look.

Anything is worth sending: a name, a rough date, a photograph with something written on the back, the name of the village people used at home. Several things here have been solved with less than that.
